# Learning Pedagogy — Overview and Glossary

This file provides theoretical orientation and a glossary for the four synthesis files in this folder. Read this first if you are new to the cognitive science of learning; skip to a specific topic file if you already know the landscape.

## Theoretical orientation

The agent-index covers four foundational research lines in the cognitive science of learning:

1. **Retrieval practice and spacing** (`01_retrieval_and_spacing.md`) — How the act of retrieving information from memory, and the schedule on which retrieval is practiced, govern long-term retention. Foundational work by Roediger, Karpicke, Cepeda, Pashler, Rohrer, and Bjork.

2. **Cognitive load and multimedia learning** (`02_cognitive_load_and_multimedia.md`) — How limited working-memory capacity constrains instructional design, and how multimedia (text + figures) can be designed to respect those constraints. Foundational work by Sweller, Kalyuga, and Mayer.

3. **Expertise development and learning outcomes** (`03_expertise_and_outcomes.md`) — How learners progress from novice to expert (deliberate practice, chunking, pattern recognition) and how to specify what learners should be able to do (revised Bloom's taxonomy). Foundational work by Ericsson, Chase & Simon, Anderson, and Krathwohl.

4. **Active strategies and technique efficacy** (`04_active_strategies.md`) — How active study strategies (self-explanation, elaboration) compare against passive ones (rereading, highlighting), and the negative result on the learning-styles hypothesis. Foundational work by Chi, Dunlosky, and Pashler.

## How these lines connect

The four lines compose into a coherent learning-design framework: retrieval and spacing govern *when* and *how often* learners should revisit material; cognitive load and multimedia govern *what an individual study episode should look like*; expertise development and learning outcomes govern *what learners should be able to do at each stage*; active strategies and technique efficacy govern *which study moves actually pay off*.

## Glossary

- **Testing effect** (also "retrieval-practice effect"): the finding that retrieving information from memory produces better long-term retention than equivalent restudying. Roediger & Karpicke (2006).

- **Spacing effect** (also "distributed practice effect"): the finding that learning sessions distributed across time produce better long-term retention than the same total study time massed into a single session. Cepeda et al. (2006).

- **Interleaving**: practicing multiple skills or problem types in a mixed order rather than blocking them by type. Often produces worse short-term performance but better long-term retention than blocking. Rohrer & Taylor (2007).

- **Desirable difficulties**: training conditions that slow apparent acquisition but improve long-term retention and transfer. Includes spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice, and variability of practice. Bjork (1994).

- **Intrinsic cognitive load**: the inherent complexity of the material being learned (element interactivity). Not directly reducible by instructional design. Sweller (1988).

- **Extraneous cognitive load**: cognitive load imposed by the instructional design itself rather than the material. Reducible by better design. Sweller (1988).

- **Germane cognitive load**: cognitive load devoted to schema construction and automation. The form of load that produces learning. Sweller (1988).

- **Expertise reversal effect**: the finding that instructional supports that help novices (e.g., worked examples, redundant verbal explanations) can hinder experts. Kalyuga et al. (2003).

- **Modality principle**: the multimedia-learning principle that on-screen text should be replaced with narration when paired with an animation or figure, because text and figure compete for the same visual channel. Mayer (2009).

- **Redundancy principle**: the multimedia-learning principle that on-screen text duplicating spoken narration tends to harm rather than help learning. Mayer & Moreno (2003).

- **Coherence principle**: the multimedia-learning principle that extraneous material (interesting but off-topic content, decorative graphics, background music) impairs learning even when learners enjoy it. Mayer (2009).

- **Signaling principle**: the multimedia-learning principle that explicit cues highlighting the organization of material (headers, typographic emphasis, outline cues) improve learning. Mayer (2009).

- **Deliberate practice**: specific, effortful, goal-directed practice with immediate feedback aimed at improving identified weaknesses. Ericsson, Krampe, & Tesch-Römer (1993).

- **Chunking** (in the expertise literature): grouping individual elements into higher-order meaningful units, the basis for expert pattern recognition. Chase & Simon (1973).

- **Revised Bloom's taxonomy**: a two-dimensional matrix of cognitive process (Remember / Understand / Apply / Analyze / Evaluate / Create) and knowledge type (factual / conceptual / procedural / metacognitive). Anderson, Krathwohl et al. (2001).

- **Self-explanation effect**: the finding that learners who generate explanations while studying worked examples acquire deeper understanding than those who do not. Chi et al. (1989).

- **Learning styles hypothesis**: the claim that matching instruction to a learner's preferred modality (visual / auditory / kinesthetic) improves learning. Lacks supporting evidence under the specific experimental design required to test it. Pashler et al. (2008).

- **High-utility learning techniques**: in Dunlosky et al. (2013), practice testing and distributed practice receive high-utility ratings based on cross-domain efficacy.

- **Low-utility learning techniques**: in Dunlosky et al. (2013), highlighting, rereading, and summarization receive low-utility ratings.
